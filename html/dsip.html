Delta Sleep Inducing Peptide (DSIP)

100–500 mcg SC or intranasally near bedtime

Delta sleep-inducing peptide (DSIP) is a synthetic nine-amino-acid peptide based on material originally isolated from rabbit cerebral venous blood in slow-wave-sleep experiments. [1,2] It is investigated for sleep continuity. Small human IV studies have produced mixed sleep findings. [4–7,10,13,30] DSIP’s endogenous identity and specific receptor remain unresolved. [19] Experimental findings implicate neuroendocrine signaling, inhibitory–excitatory balance, and cellular stress responses. [12,18,20]

💉💉 Brief IV administration was generally well tolerated in small insomnia studies. Cardiovascular reactions in other clinical settings and the separate risks of repeated SC exposure require endpoint-specific assessment. [21,23]

Deep sleep, also called slow-wave sleep or stage N3, is the deepest non-rapid eye movement (non-REM) stage and is difficult to interrupt. It is associated with restorative processes; the amount of slow-wave sleep is also associated with growth hormone secretion. [26,27] Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep features rapid eye movements, dreaming, and temporary inhibition of arm and leg muscle activity. Both REM and non-REM sleep contribute to normal sleep cycling and memory processes. Lighter non-REM stages precede deep sleep. [26]

Small human studies observed tolerability of slowly administered IV DSIP over brief treatment periods. [4–7,13,23] This finding is distinct from longer-term hepatic, renal, cardiovascular, immunological, reproductive, and proliferative outcomes.

Human evidence for sleep improvement is mixed. Several small early IV studies reported improvements in sleep duration, efficiency, or continuity. [4–6] The two 1981 insomnia reports describe the same patient cohort and should not be counted as independent replication. [3,5,23] The positive 1987 study used seven successive IV treatments and compared results with baseline and external healthy controls; it did not include a concurrent randomized placebo-treatment arm. [10,23] A separate 1987 double-blind crossover study found sleep changes of little clinical significance. [30] The 1992 double-blind study found weak objective changes and no subjective sleep-quality benefit. Overall efficacy remains inconclusive. [13]

Dosing Evidence and Patterns

DSIP degrades rapidly. [11] Early human sleep studies used slow intravenous (IV) administration and reported that administration rate influenced the observed response. [6,8] Subcutaneous (SC) administration could plausibly slow absorption relative to IV administration.

Historical human experiments used slowly administered IV DSIP at 25 nmol/kg. [4–6,13,30] With a molecular weight of approximately 848.8 g/mol, this equals about 21.2 mcg/kg, or approximately 1.5 mg for a 70-kg person. [2,23]

An early review described U-shaped relationships between DSIP effects and dose. This pattern makes a simple assumption that more exposure produces more benefit unreliable. [8]

Human SC bioavailability and PK have not been established for DSIP. [23] As a speculative sensitivity model, assuming 30%–80% SC bioavailability would make approximately 1.9–5 mg SC arithmetically equivalent to 1.5 mg IV in total systemic exposure, if clearance were also unchanged. The 30%–80% range is an assumed input, not a measured DSIP value.

Published human studies examined short IV regimens including three, four, and seven consecutive treatment nights or afternoons. [6,10,13,30] A small open study administered ten injections to seven patients. [7]

Speculative Experimental Dosing

In spite of the larger IV doses used in studies, the typical practice found today is a much smaller dose, typically taken in intranasally before bedtime.

• 100–500 mcg SC or intranasally near bedtime

Continuous use and cycling are both speculative experimental schedules discussed.

Human IV studies reported effects beginning after approximately one to two hours and, in some experiments, delayed effects on subsequent sleep. [4–6] A slower absorption profile after SC administration is plausible; a specific pre-sleep interval, onset time, or duration for SC or intranasal use remains speculative.

Benefits

Reported effects vary substantially across small human studies, from measurable sleep improvements to weak effects of limited clinical significance. [4–7,10,13,30] Differences in study design, population, administration route, dose, and timing could contribute to this variation; as could placebo effects in uncontrolled, unblinded studies. [8,23]

  • Sleep architecture: Effects are inconclusive. Some human studies reported improved sleep efficiency, latency, or continuity, while controlled findings were weak in other studies. [4–6,10,13,30] Increased delta activity in rats does not establish increased human slow-wave sleep; effects vary across experimental systems. [8,16,19]
  • Neuroendocrine modulation: IV DSIP reduced plasma adrenocorticotropic hormone-like immunoreactivity (ACTH-LI) for at least three hours in a randomized crossover, without changing cortisol. Broader modulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) function and stress-related arousal is plausible. [12]
  • Daytime function: Small human studies reported improved daytime alertness or performance after IV DSIP, although the positive 1987 study lacked a concurrent placebo-treatment group. [6,10,23] Mood improvement was reported in the seven-patient open study. [7]

Potential Applications

  • Insomnia or fragmented sleep
  • Stress-related sleep disruption
  • Recovery support
  • Non-benzodiazepine sleep research

Cautions

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Reproductive, developmental, and lactation safety remain insufficiently characterized. Estrous-cycle observations in adult mice do not establish pregnancy or infant safety. [23,25]
  • Severe or unstable psychiatric illness: Effects on psychiatric symptoms remain insufficiently characterized; changes in arousal and neuroendocrine signaling make extrapolation from small insomnia studies inappropriate. [12,21,23]
  • Concurrent sedative-hypnotics, central nervous system depressants, or other investigational sleep agents: Altered sedation, arousal, or sleep architecture is plausible. The direction may differ by agent: DSIP lightened isoflurane anesthesia in a small randomized study, rather than uniformly increasing sedation. [21]
  • Severe hepatic or renal impairment: The effects of impairment on DSIP exposure and clearance remain insufficiently characterized. [23]

Reported and Possible Side Effects

Small human IV insomnia studies generally described DSIP as well tolerated during brief exposure. These observations concern the monitored populations and routes. [4–7,13,23]

  • Headache, nausea, vertigo, perspiration, and hypotension: In an older IV withdrawal-treatment series of 107 patients, nine experienced minor transient effects and three had more serious reactions, including two cases of hypotension. One patient developed progressive hypotension after a second injection. The withdrawal setting and limited reporting complicate causal attribution and do not establish event rates for SC or intranasal use. [23]
  • Autonomic changes during anesthesia: In a randomized study of 24 women, IV DSIP increased heart rate and reduced heart-rate variability during isoflurane anesthesia, alongside EEG and bispectral-index findings consistent with lighter anesthesia. [21]

Use with Low Dose Epitalon

The speculative, experimental low-dose Epitalon schedule discussed for sleep is

• 50–300 mcg intranasally before bed

Epitalon has a plausible mechanistic connection to circadian regulation: experiments reported altered pineal markers in stressed rats and increased evening melatonin in old monkeys. [17,22,29] However, it did not significantly alter melatonin secretion in isolated pineal glands from young or old rats, indicating context-dependent effects. [28] Sleep benefits from low-dose nightly administration remain speculative.

Combining low-dose DSIP with low-dose Epitalon is a speculative experimental practice.

Biological Mechanism

DSIP-like immunoreactivity has been detected in mammalian tissues and fluids, but the identity, synthesis, and physiological role of an endogenous DSIP molecule remain unresolved. A specific receptor has not been identified. [9,19] Experimental studies report effects on sleep-related electrophysiology, neuroendocrine signaling, cellular stress responses, and autonomic activity. [12,16,18,20,21] These findings support several plausible, context-dependent mechanisms rather than a single validated pathway.

Neural signaling: In rat neurons, DSIP potentiated GABA-activated currents and modified N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA)/glutamate signaling. [20] Rat EEG studies reported increased delta-frequency activity. [16] Modulation of inhibitory–excitatory balance is therefore a plausible mechanism for sleep-related effects in humans.

Stress-axis signaling: A small randomized human crossover study found decreased ACTH-like immunoreactivity with unchanged cortisol. [12] This supports a specific neuroendocrine effect, rather than a demonstrated reduction in adrenal cortisol output. Broader HPA modulation remains a plausible mechanism for stress-related effects.

Cellular stress and neuroprotection: In specific rat models, DSIP reduced neurological impairment and mortality after cerebral ischemia and preserved brain-mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation during hypoxia. [14,18] Rat studies also reported altered glutamate signaling [20] and lipid-peroxidation markers under hyperoxic conditions. [15] A later intranasal rat stroke study found motor improvement over time within the DSIP group, but no significant reduction in infarct volume or rotation asymmetry versus controls. [24] Neuroprotection is therefore supported in particular preclinical settings; broader human recovery and resilience benefits remain plausible. [31]

Autonomic signaling: IV DSIP increased heart rate and reduced heart-rate variability during isoflurane anesthesia, findings consistent with reduced parasympathetic activity in that setting. [21] Effects on autonomic regulation during ordinary sleep are plausible.
