AC-SDKP is N-acetyl-seryl-aspartyl-lysyl-proline, a naturally occurring four-amino-acid peptide also called goralatide. It corresponds to residues 1 through 4 of thymosin beta 4. Meprin-alpha and prolyl oligopeptidase participate in its release from thymosin beta 4, and the N-domain of angiotensin-converting enzyme rapidly degrades it. Its reported circulating half-life is very short at approximately 4.5 minutes.

How AC-SDKP Fits Within Thymosin Beta 4 and TB-500 Biology

Full-length thymosin beta 4 contains multiple biologically active regions. AC-SDKP is the N-terminal 1-4 fragment associated primarily with antifibrotic, anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and hematopoietic-regulatory activity. TB-500 is a different fragment, Ac-LKKTETQ, corresponding to residues 17 through 23 and centered on an actin-binding region. Evidence for TB-500 should not be attributed to AC-SDKP, and evidence for AC-SDKP should not be attributed to TB-500.

AC-SDKP is unusual among gray-market peptides because it is also an endogenous human metabolite and a recognized substrate of angiotensin-converting enzyme. ACE inhibitors raise plasma AC-SDKP concentrations several-fold in humans. This confirms human exposure to elevated endogenous AC-SDKP but does not establish the efficacy of administering synthetic AC-SDKP.

Dosing Patterns

No evidence-based human therapeutic dose or subcutaneous dosing interval has been established. The peptide degrades rapidly and most successful animal studies used continuous infusion by osmotic minipump rather than intermittent injections.

Translating those regimens into community injection schedules is speculative.

Documented Clinical Exposure

Historical goralatide trial: A randomized oncology trial used 12.5 or 62.5 micrograms/kg/day on days 1 through 4 with carboplatin and fluorouracil. The study evaluated myeloprotection, not fibrosis treatment, and did not show hematologic benefit.

Common Animal Research Exposure

Continuous infusion: Many rat studies used approximately 400 to 1,600 micrograms/kg/day delivered continuously by osmotic minipump.

Combination Context

ACE inhibitors reduce AC-SDKP degradation and substantially raise circulating concentrations in humans. Concurrent synthetic AC-SDKP and ACE inhibition could plausibly increase or prolong exposure, while the magnitude and clinical consequences are unknown. In a 5xFAD mouse study, AC-SDKP was administered with TB-500 and the combination reduced neuroinflammatory and neuronal injury markers; the individual contribution of AC-SDKP was not isolated.

Benefits

The most reproducible AC-SDKP findings involve limiting maladaptive inflammation, fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation, and excess extracellular-matrix deposition. The evidence supports likely organ-protective effects in the animal injury models studied, while human therapeutic benefits remain speculative.

  • Cardiac antifibrotic effects: AC-SDKP is likely to reduce cardiac inflammation, macrophage infiltration, and interstitial and perivascular fibrosis in hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, and radiation injury models. Functional improvement varies by model.
  • Renal protection: AC-SDKP is likely to reduce renal inflammation and fibrosis and may improve proteinuria or renal function in animal models of nephritis, reduced renal mass, ureteral obstruction, and diabetic nephropathy.
  • Hepatic antifibrotic effects: AC-SDKP is likely to reduce hepatic inflammation, stellate-cell activation, collagen deposition, and TGF-beta/Smad signaling in animal models of chronic liver injury.
  • Pulmonary antifibrotic effects: AC-SDKP is likely to reduce collagen deposition, myofibroblast differentiation, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in animal models of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Inflammatory signaling regulation: AC-SDKP is likely to reduce macrophage infiltration and inflammatory signaling in injured heart, kidney, liver, and lung tissue based on animal evidence.
  • Angiogenesis: AC-SDKP is likely to stimulate endothelial migration, capillary-like structure formation, and new-vessel growth based on in-vitro and animal evidence.
  • Hematopoietic progenitor quiescence: AC-SDKP reversibly suppresses proliferation of primitive hematopoietic progenitors in experimental systems. Clinical myeloprotection during chemotherapy remains speculative because a randomized trial found no meaningful hematologic benefit.
  • Neuroinflammatory support: Reduced neuroinflammation, neurite atrophy, neuronal apoptosis, and memory impairment are plausible based on a combined AC-SDKP and TB-500 study in cell models and 5xFAD mice.

Potential Applications

  • Cardiac remodeling: AC-SDKP likely limits maladaptive inflammation and fibrosis after myocardial infarction, hypertension, diabetes, or cardiac irradiation in animal models.
  • Progressive kidney fibrosis: AC-SDKP likely reduces renal inflammatory and fibrotic remodeling in several animal models. Human efficacy for chronic kidney disease remains speculative.
  • Chronic liver fibrosis: AC-SDKP likely reduces fibrogenic signaling and collagen accumulation in experimental liver injury. Human efficacy remains speculative.
  • Fibrotic lung disease: AC-SDKP likely reduces silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rats. Benefits for human silicosis or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis remain speculative.
  • Ischemic tissue repair: AC-SDKP plausibly supports microvascular repair through pro-angiogenic endothelial effects, while benefits depend on tissue and disease context.
  • Bone-marrow protection: Protection of quiescent hematopoietic progenitors from selected cytotoxic injury is plausible from experimental evidence, while clinical myeloprotection remains speculative.

Cautions

  • Active or previous malignancy: Pro-angiogenic activity creates a plausible concern that AC-SDKP could support vascularization or progression of an existing malignancy. Its effects on cancer initiation and recurrence are speculative.
  • Anemia or marrow suppression: AC-SDKP can inhibit primitive hematopoietic progenitor proliferation. Anemia and lymphopenia were more frequent in one short randomized oncology trial, and very high endogenous levels have been associated with erythropoietin resistance in renal failure.
  • ACE inhibitor use: ACE inhibitors markedly increase endogenous AC-SDKP concentrations. Combined exposure could plausibly increase duration or magnitude of effect.
  • Impaired renal function: Renal failure can substantially increase AC-SDKP accumulation, especially during ACE inhibition.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding is unknown. Avoid use as a precaution.

Side Effects

  • Short clinical exposure: A four-day randomized oncology trial described overall clinical and biological tolerability as excellent, but occurrences of anemia and lymphopenia increased.

Potential for Diminishing Benefit

AC-SDKP is not established as a classical receptor agonist, and receptor desensitization has not been demonstrated. Its unusually short circulating half-life makes rapid enzymatic clearance a more immediate limitation than pharmacological tolerance.

Biological Mechanism

AC-SDKP is released from the N-terminus of thymosin beta 4 through sequential proteolysis involving meprin-alpha and prolyl oligopeptidase. It is then hydrolyzed predominantly by the N-domain of ACE. ACE inhibition therefore raises endogenous AC-SDKP concentrations without requiring direct peptide administration.

Its antifibrotic activity is linked to reduced TGF-beta production or signaling, lower Smad2/3 and ERK1/2 activation, preservation or induction of inhibitory Smad7, reduced connective-tissue growth factor signaling, and suppression of fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation. These pathways plausibly explain the repeated reductions in collagen and fibronectin deposition across organs.

Its anti-inflammatory effects include reduced macrophage infiltration, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 activity, NF-kappa B signaling, and inflammatory cytokine expression in experimental injury. AC-SDKP also acts directly on endothelial cells to promote migration, tube formation, and angiogenesis.

In hematopoiesis, AC-SDKP acts as a reversible negative regulator that keeps primitive progenitors out of the cell cycle. This can plausibly protect quiescent progenitors from cell-cycle-dependent injury, but excessive or sustained exposure could also suppress erythropoietic recovery.

AC-SDKP and Cancer Risk

AC-SDKP stimulates endothelial migration and angiogenesis in experimental models. Because tumors can depend on neovascularization, support of existing tumor growth or progression is a plausible concern. AC-SDKP also alters hematopoietic progenitor cycling and has context-dependent effects in tumor and leukemia models. Its net effect on human cancer initiation, progression, treatment response, and recurrence remains speculative.

Conclusions

Short-term tolerability is likely for brief clinical exposure, while long-term safety is plausible. ACE inhibitor use, impaired renal function, anemia or marrow suppression, and malignancy materially change the biological context and warrant particular caution.

AC-SDKP is likely to reduce pathological inflammation and fibrosis in cardiac, renal, hepatic, and pulmonary animal models. Pro-angiogenic and hematopoietic-regulatory effects are also experimentally supported. Therapeutic human efficacy, intermittent subcutaneous dosing, and community cycling practices remain speculative.
