Retatrutide Available for Research Use Only

(2, 4, 6, 9, 12 mg / week, SC)

Retatrutide is a once-weekly triple agonist of the GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon receptors. Randomized human trials show substantial dose-dependent weight loss, improved glycemic control, reduced total and visceral fat, reduced liver fat, and improvements in several cardiometabolic biomarkers. Its glucagon-receptor activity likely contributes to increased fatty-acid oxidation and energy expenditure. Safety and tolerability through approximately 80 weeks are supported by human trials, while longer-term cardiovascular outcomes remain under study.

Retatrutide is a single peptide with long half-life that activates the GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon receptors. GLP-1 and GIP receptor activity reduces appetite and improves glucose-dependent insulin secretion, while glucagon-receptor activity promotes hepatic fatty-acid oxidation and likely increases energy expenditure. These combined actions support substantial weight loss and broad metabolic improvement.

Retatrutide has been evaluated in randomized human trials lasting up to 80 weeks. Its safety profile is dominated by dose-related gastrointestinal effects, particularly nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting, and decreased appetite. Dysesthesia has also occurred in Phase 3 trials, particularly at higher doses, and modest increases in resting heart rate were observed in Phase 2. Longer-term safety and cardiovascular outcomes remain under study.

Retatrutide produces large, dose-dependent reductions in body weight. Participants receiving the highest studied doses lost an average of 24.2% at 48 weeks in the Phase 2 obesity trial, 28.7% at 68 weeks in TRIUMPH-4, and 22.6% at 80 weeks in TRIUMPH-3.

Retatrutide improves glycemic control and multiple cardiometabolic markers. Human trials show reductions in hemoglobin A1c, triglycerides, non-HDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, inflammatory biomarkers, and metabolic markers associated with insulin resistance.

Retatrutide substantially reduces visceral adipose tissue. MRI-based human data show dose-related reductions in visceral fat together with reductions in abdominal subcutaneous fat, liver fat, body weight, and waist circumference.

Dosing Patterns

In Phase 3 trials, it is administered subcutaneously once weekly, beginning at 2 mg weekly and increasing every four weeks until the assigned maintenance dose is reached. Studied Phase 3 maintenance doses are 4 mg, 9 mg, and 12 mg. The escalation sequence is 2 mg to 4 mg for the 4 mg target; 2 mg to 4 mg to 6 mg to 9 mg for the 9 mg target; and 2 mg to 4 mg to 6 mg to 9 mg to 12 mg for the 12 mg target.

Gastrointestinal adverse effects are dose-related. Phase 2 findings indicate that beginning at 2 mg rather than 4 mg can improve tolerability. The four-week interval between increases allows exposure to approach steady state and provides time to assess tolerability before further escalation.

Speculative community patterns

Starting at 1 mg, increasing in smaller increments than those used in trials, and dividing the weekly amount into two injections are speculative community practices to improve tolerability.

Some community users reduce the dose or continue a lower maintenance dose after reaching their weight goal. The optimal maintenance dose and tapering method remain under study.

Benefits

  • Appetite reduction: Retatrutide reduces appetite and likely reduces energy intake. Greater appetite suppression than semaglutide or tirzepatide remains speculative, although community reports frequently describe marked reductions in hunger, cravings, and food noise.
  • Visceral-fat reduction: Retatrutide substantially reduces visceral adipose tissue in humans. Its glucagon-receptor activity likely contributes through increased fatty-acid oxidation and energy expenditure.
  • Energy expenditure: Retatrutide likely increases energy expenditure based on animal evidence, supported by human biomarker findings showing increased fatty-acid oxidation. The magnitude of this effect in humans remains uncertain.
  • Metabolic flexibility: Improved metabolic flexibility is plausible based on human biomarker changes associated with increased fatty-acid oxidation and reduced insulin resistance.
  • Glycemic control: Retatrutide improves glycemic control, including reductions in hemoglobin A1c and fasting glucose.
  • Reduced progression to type 2 diabetes: Retatrutide is likely to reduce progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes. Its substantial effects on body weight, insulin sensitivity, fasting glucose, and hemoglobin A1c support this inference.
  • Insulin sensitivity: Retatrutide improves insulin sensitivity, with reductions in fasting insulin and multiple metabolic biomarkers associated with insulin resistance.
  • Early weight loss: Retatrutide produces substantial early weight loss that continues with ongoing treatment. Faster early weight loss than semaglutide or tirzepatide remains speculative, although it is frequently described in community reports.
  • Physical function and quality of life: Retatrutide improves knee-osteoarthritis pain and physical function in adults with obesity. Broader improvements in weight-related mobility, physical function, and quality of life are likely.
  • Obstructive sleep apnea: Retatrutide is likely to improve obesity-related obstructive sleep apnea through substantial reductions in body weight and visceral fat.
  • Liver-fat reduction: Retatrutide produces large, dose-related reductions in liver fat. In a randomized human substudy, mean relative liver-fat reduction at 24 weeks ranged from 42.9% with 1 mg to 82.4% with 12 mg.
  • Metabolic liver disease progression: Retatrutide plausibly improves MASH and slows progression of metabolic liver disease through its large reductions in liver fat, body weight, insulin resistance, and inflammatory biomarkers.
  • Cardiometabolic markers: Retatrutide reduces systolic blood pressure, triglycerides, non-HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, atherogenic lipoprotein particles, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and interleukin-6.
  • Cardiovascular risk reduction: Retatrutide is likely to reduce cardiovascular risk through its substantial improvements in body weight, glycemic control, blood pressure, atherogenic lipoproteins, triglycerides, and inflammatory biomarkers.
  • Kidney protection: Retatrutide is likely to reduce long-term kidney risk through improvements in glycemic control, blood pressure, body weight, and metabolic inflammation.
  • Microvascular protection: Retatrutide is likely to reduce long-term retinal, neuropathic, and other microvascular complications through sustained improvements in glycemic control and related metabolic risk factors.

Studied Applications

  • Obesity and overweight: Weight reduction in adults with obesity or overweight and weight-related health conditions.
  • Type 2 diabetes: Glycemic control and weight reduction in adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity.
  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: Reduction of liver fat and associated metabolic abnormalities.
  • Obesity-related conditions: Weight reduction and related functional outcomes in people with obesity and knee osteoarthritis or obstructive sleep apnea.
  • Cardiovascular and kidney outcomes: Retatrutide improves multiple cardiovascular risk markers and kidney parameters.

Potential Applications

  • Alcohol craving and intake: Reduced alcohol craving and consumption with retatrutide are plausible through GLP-1-mediated effects on appetite and reward pathways.
  • Nicotine craving: Reduced nicotine craving with retatrutide is plausible through shared GLP-1 receptor activity and effects on reward-related pathways.
  • Neuroprotective and cognitive effects: Neuroprotective effects from retatrutide are plausible based on shared incretin biology, while clinically meaningful cognitive benefits remain speculative.

Cautions

  • Medullary thyroid carcinoma and MEN2: Avoid retatrutide in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2. This precaution is based on thyroid C-cell tumors observed with GLP-1 receptor agonism in rodents; a causal human thyroid-cancer risk has not been established.
  • Pancreatic and gallbladder disease: Use caution in people with a history of pancreatitis or gallbladder disease. Acute pancreatitis and gallbladder events have occurred with GLP-1-based therapies, and substantial or rapid weight loss can increase gallstone risk.
  • Severe gastrointestinal motility disorders: Use caution in people with severe gastroparesis or other significant gastrointestinal motility disorders. GLP-1 receptor activation slows gastric emptying, and retatrutide commonly produces dose-related gastrointestinal effects.
  • Volume depletion and kidney injury: Persistent nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea can cause dehydration and acute kidney injury. Maintaining hydration is particularly important during dose escalation and for people with kidney disease or other risk factors for volume depletion.
  • Insulin and sulfonylureas: Concurrent use with insulin or sulfonylureas can increase hypoglycemia risk. Dose adjustment of those medications may be necessary under qualified clinical guidance.
  • Diabetic retinopathy: Rapid improvement in glucose control can temporarily worsen diabetic retinopathy. People with a history of diabetic retinopathy should be monitored for progression or new visual symptoms.
  • Oral hormonal contraceptives: Retatrutide may plausibly reduce the absorption and effectiveness of oral hormonal contraceptives by delaying gastric emptying, particularly during treatment initiation and dose escalation. A nonoral method or additional barrier contraception may be considered until retatrutide-specific interaction data are available.
  • Anesthesia and deep sedation: Retatrutide’s delayed gastric emptying plausibly increases the risk of retained stomach contents and pulmonary aspiration during general anesthesia or deep sedation. Inform the anesthesia or procedural team about current retatrutide use before a planned procedure.

Side Effects

  • Gastrointestinal effects: Documented effects include nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting, decreased appetite, abdominal discomfort, abdominal distension, indigestion, gastroesophageal reflux, and belching. These effects are generally dose-related. A lower starting dose and gradual escalation, with dose increases no more frequently than every four weeks, improve tolerability.
  • Fatigue: Fatigue occurred in retatrutide trials, including rates of approximately 6% to 7% in some dose groups.
  • Hair shedding: Temporary hair shedding is plausible during rapid or substantial weight loss with retatrutide, particularly when protein or micronutrient intake is inadequate. This would most often reflect weight-loss-associated telogen effluvium rather than a known direct retatrutide effect.
  • Dysesthesia: Altered skin sensations, including tingling, burning, tenderness, or heightened sensitivity, have occurred in Phase 3 trials and appear more common at higher doses.
  • Resting heart rate: Modest dose-dependent increases in resting heart rate occurred in Phase 2, peaked during treatment, and later declined.

Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Retatrutide should be avoided during pregnancy, while attempting to conceive, and while breastfeeding. Pregnant and breastfeeding participants have been excluded from retatrutide clinical trials, and human pregnancy and lactation data specific to retatrutide are unavailable.

Observational evidence involving other GLP-1-based drugs provides limited reassurance that inadvertent exposure around conception or early pregnancy is not consistently associated with major congenital malformations. This indirect evidence does not establish the safety of continued retatrutide use during pregnancy.

A retatrutide-specific preconception washout interval has not been established. Pregnancy planning should include discontinuation sufficiently in advance under qualified clinical guidance.

Retatrutide vs Tirzepatide

Tirzepatide activates the GIP and GLP-1 receptors. Retatrutide activates those same receptors and additionally activates the glucagon receptor, creating a distinct metabolic profile.

Retatrutide likely produces greater glucagon-driven stimulation of fatty-acid oxidation and energy expenditure than tirzepatide. This distinction is supported by receptor biology and animal evidence, together with human retatrutide biomarker findings consistent with increased fatty-acid oxidation. Tirzepatide’s effects are driven more predominantly by appetite reduction, improved glycemic control, and increased fat oxidation without a demonstrated increase in human energy expenditure.

Claims that retatrutide produces greater human weight loss, visceral-fat reduction, liver-fat reduction, endurance, or cardiometabolic benefit than tirzepatide are mechanistically likely, but are without direct comparative results. Community reports frequently describe faster fat loss, easier fasting, and a different subjective energy profile with retatrutide.

Retatrutide to Support Body Recomposition

Retatrutide improves body composition by producing large reductions in total fat mass. Human Phase 2 data also show some lean-mass loss, with the proportion of lean mass lost being similar to that observed with other potent weight-loss treatments. Glucagon-receptor activity and increased fatty-acid oxidation provide a plausible mechanism for preferential fat loss, but a retatrutide-specific muscle-sparing effect remains speculative. Adequate protein intake and resistance training may help preserve lean mass during weight loss.

Biological Mechanism

Retatrutide activates GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon receptors, producing coordinated effects on appetite regulation, insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and energy expenditure. GLP-1 and GIP signaling reduce appetite, improve glycemic control, and enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, while glucagon-receptor activation promotes fatty-acid oxidation and likely increases energy expenditure. Together, these pathways produce large reductions in body weight and improvements in metabolic markers.
