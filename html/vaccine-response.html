I Tried Thymosin α1 to Boost My Vaccinations

I was due for my annual fall vaccinations: the updated Moderna COVID-19 vaccine and the senior influenza vaccine. I decided to try a small experiment with thymosin α1 (Tα1) to see whether it might enhance my immune response.

Hypothesis: Tα1 administration around vaccination may improve immune response

Tα1 is a peptide I use weekly or occasionally as part of my regular stack. It has immunomodulatory effects, including influencing dendritic-cell function and T-cell responses. Rather than simply stimulating immunity, Tα1 can influence both immune activation and regulatory pathways. [4]

There is also human evidence that Tα1 can enhance vaccine-induced antibody responses. In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial involving 90 older men, Tα1 improved antibody responses to influenza vaccination, with the benefit concentrated in participants aged 77 and older. The protocol involved eight injections over four weeks. (Although unplanned, my regular 1.6 mg weekly dose is approximately comparable to the study's doses administered twice weekly for four weeks after vaccination.) A separate pilot study in hemodialysis patients also reported improved influenza vaccine immunogenicity with Tα1 administration. [1,2]

More recently, an observational study found that higher naturally circulating Tα1 levels were associated with slower waning of COVID-19 vaccine antibodies over eight months. This doesn't establish that administering Tα1 improves COVID-19 vaccine responses, but it provides additional biological support for the hypothesis, and biological support for my weekly Tα1. [3]

These studies involved repeated dosing or measured endogenous Tα1. My approach of taking 1.6 mg four hours before vaccination, against a background of regular weekly Tα1 use, is therefore a mechanistic extrapolation, rather than an established vaccine-enhancement protocol.

Hypothesis: Restorative peptides might interfere with vaccine response

I also decided to suspend several of my restorative peptides for 72 hours: KPV, GHK-Cu, BPC-157, and ARA-290. These peptides have demonstrated anti-inflammatory or tissue-protective activity in cell and animal studies, including effects on inflammatory cytokines and signaling pathways. [5–8]

Early innate immune activation and inflammatory signaling contribute to vaccine-induced immune responses. I wondered whether the anti-inflammatory effects of these peptides might dampen some of that signaling, potentially interfering with vaccine immunogenicity.

However, anti-inflammatory activity does not automatically imply suppression of beneficial vaccine responses. The effects are pathway-dependent, and modulating inflammation can sometimes improve rather than impair immune function.

My decision to suspend these peptides was just precautionary and speculative. I found no direct evidence that these peptides impair vaccine immunogenicity or that temporarily suspending them improves it.

The experiment

I was curious whether I would experience a noticeably different reaction to the vaccines. Unfortunately, I don't have access to the laboratory testing needed to evaluate my actual immune response. All I can observe is how I feel.

My usual response to vaccinations varies. Often, I have no noticeable reaction at all. Occasionally, I experience a few hours of low-grade fever and body aches.

I took 1.6 mg of Tα1 at approximately 9 AM, followed by both vaccinations at 1 PM.

I continued with a fairly normal day, including my usual lower-body resistance workout at the gym, some shopping, and an intentionally easy evening.

At approximately 9 PM, eight hours after vaccination, I began feeling unusually warm and developed mild body aches. These symptoms continued through bedtime. I slept reasonably well, although the aches were noticeable throughout the night.

When I woke at my normal time the next day, the aches were still present. I spent a quiet morning mostly watching television, and the symptoms gradually subsided over the day.

What does this tell me? Anything?

The timing and symptoms are consistent with a normal systemic reaction to vaccination. Studies show that receiving influenza and COVID-19 vaccines together can modestly increase the likelihood of systemic symptoms, including muscle aches and fatigue. [9]

The more interesting question is whether my reaction indicates a stronger immune response.

Not necessarily.

Muscle aches and fatigue are consistent with inflammatory responses to vaccination, but these symptoms are not reliable individual measures of adaptive immunity. In one prospective study of COVID-19 vaccine recipients, researchers found no significant correlation between the severity of postvaccination symptoms and subsequent antibody titers. [10]

My experience also involved several simultaneous variables: Tα1 administration, two vaccinations, temporary suspension of restorative peptides, and a resistance workout. Without a control condition or immune-response measurements, I cannot determine whether any of these contributed to the symptoms beyond the vaccinations themselves.

So, what did I learn?

I experienced a noticeable but mild systemic reaction following Tα1 administration and two vaccinations. The reaction was somewhat more pronounced than what I usually experience, but still well within the range of normal vaccine responses.

The published evidence provides a plausible rationale for investigating Tα1 as a vaccine-response enhancer, particularly in older adults. However, my observation provides no direct evidence that it enhanced my immunity.

The hypothesis remains plausible. The experiment was interesting. The result is inconclusive.
