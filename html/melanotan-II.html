Melanotan II (MT‑II)

100–250 mcg SC daily; 250–500 mcg SC daily or 1–2 times weekly

Melanotan II (MTII) has demonstrated skin-darkening and erectile effects in small human studies, with appetite reduction also reported during experimental administration. [1,2,16] It is a synthetic cyclic heptapeptide analogue of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH). [1] It activates MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R signaling, with MC1R supporting pigmentation and central melanocortin pathways contributing to feeding and erectile responses. [10,11] Nausea and other systemic effects occurred in human testing. [1,2,16] Changing pigmented lesions, melanoma, and serious acute adverse events have been reported, with causal certainty differing by outcome. [5–8,17–19]

☠️Possibly unsafe; serious adverse events reported. [5–8,14,17–19]

⭐⭐ Likely effective for skin darkening (small human pilot study). [1]

Caution with a history of melanoma or atypical moles; avoid unsupervised use with active cancer. This is precautionary advice, not proof that MTII promotes every cancer. [5,6,9,14]

Nausea, fatigue, and erectile effects were observed during short human experiments. [1,2,16] Serious events after nonmedical exposure include priapism and systemic toxicity with muscle and kidney injury. [7,8] Melanoma reports constitute a dermatologic safety signal, not proof of carcinogenicity or a numerical risk estimate. [5,6]

Skin darkening was measured in a very small pilot study, while erections were documented in a small placebo-controlled trial. [1,2]

Dose and Patterns

The human pigmentation pilot began at 0.01 mg/kg SC and escalated to 0.025–0.03 mg/kg, alternating MTII and saline over two weeks. [1] The organic and psychogenic erectile-dysfunction crossover studies used 0.025 mg/kg SC. [2,16] These historical exposures differ from the proposed loading and maintenance patterns above.

The following speculative experimental dose patterns are discussed in peptide communities. These patterns describe proposed nonmedical use.

  • 100–250 mcg SC daily for several days — initial pattern to test tolerability,
  • 250–500 mcg SC daily — loading pattern to achieve cosmetic effects,
  • 250–500 mcg SC once or twice weekly — maintenance pattern to maintain cosmetic effects.

Benefits

  • Likely visible skin darkening — (small human pilot) Two of three men developed increased pigmentation after five MTII injections over two weeks; pigmentation was assessed one week after completion. The sample limits estimates of consistency, magnitude, and speed. [1]
  • Plausible partial photoprotection — (mechanistic extrapolation) Increased pigmentation and melanocortin-related cellular repair provide a biologically plausible protective hypothesis. The cellular repair evidence involves α-MSH, and the human UV-response evidence below involves afamelanotide. [12,13]
  • Erections in men with erectile dysfunction — (small controlled human trials) Ten men with organic risk factors participated in a crossover study; erections occurred after 12 of 19 MTII injections versus 1 of 21 placebo injections, and sexual desire increased. [2] In a separate crossover study of ten men with psychogenic erectile dysfunction, eight developed clinically apparent erections after MTII. [16] These findings concern the studied male populations.

Investigated and Reported Uses

  • Skin darkening and erectile responses — investigated in small human studies. Cosmetic tanning is a reported nonmedical use of an unapproved product; “off-label” should not imply that MTII itself is an approved medicine. [1,2,4]

Cautions

  • Personal or family history of melanoma, or atypical moles — (precaution based on dermatologic concern) changing lesions warrant assessment. [5,6]
  • Uncontrolled cardiovascular disease — (clinical precaution) systemic toxicity with tachycardia and elevated blood pressure has been reported, including after a large dose. [7]
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding — (safety not established) the cited human studies do not support use in these populations. [1,2]

Side effects

  • Nausea, yawning, stretching, fatigue, and sleepiness — (human studies) These effects occurred during short experimental exposures. Severe nausea followed 4 of 19 MTII injections in the organic erectile-dysfunction study. [1,2,16]
  • Spontaneous or prolonged erections — (human trials and case reports) Erections may be unwanted. [1,2] A reported ischemic priapism episode required surgery after conservative measures failed, with persistent erectile impairment at 15 weeks. [8]
  • Appetite and cardiovascular effects — Decreased appetite was reported during a controlled human experiment. [16] Tachycardia and hypertension occurred in a systemic toxicity case; these observations do not define the incidence at other exposures. [7]
  • Darkening or appearance of atypical moles — (case reports) new or changing pigmented lesions have occurred after use. Melanoma has also been reported in association. [5,6]
  • Serious systemic events — Rhabdomyolysis and kidney dysfunction were documented after a 6 mg injection, with MTII confirmed in the supplied product. [7] Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) has been reported. [14,17] A renal-infarction case followed intermittent use, with the last reported injection three weeks before admission; the temporal association and product identification do not establish causation. [19]

Clinical and Regulatory Status

MTII has early human research but no established cosmetic treatment regimen. FDA identifies potential immunogenicity and serious reported adverse events in its compounding safety assessment. [4,14]

Biological mechanism

MTII is a cyclic α-MSH analogue. [1,10] It is a nonselective agonist at MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R, with receptor experiments demonstrating cyclic-AMP signaling. [10] MC2R is the ACTH-responsive receptor and requires its accessory protein MRAP for ACTH binding and signaling. [20] MC1R supports pigmentation, while central melanocortin pathways provide a biological explanation for feeding and erectile effects. [1,2,11,16]

Pigmentation and UV Protection

MTII-induced pigmentation was observed in a three-person human pilot. That study did not measure a protective SPF or establish reduced skin-cancer incidence. Pigmentation should therefore be distinguished from a quantified clinical protection level. [1]

A related compound provides separate human evidence. In nine participants, afamelanotide reduced the measured UV-erythema response, while its increase in minimal erythema dose (MED) was not statistically significant. These are findings for afamelanotide, not MTII. [13]

Plausible protection remains a mechanistic interpretation. In cultured human melanocytes, α-MSH supported antiapoptotic pathways and reduced UV-related DNA damage. [12] Ordinary sun protection, including sunscreen, protective clothing, and shade, remains appropriate regardless of increased pigmentation. [15]

Melanotan II and Cancer

MTII use has been associated with atypical moles and melanoma. These case reports cannot establish a numerical risk estimate. [5,6] Avoiding nonmedical cosmetic use is a precaution supported by the overall adverse-event concerns. [3,4,7,8,14]

Safety Evidence summary

Human dermatologic observations: Published reports include melanoma in situ and rapidly appearing atypical moles after melanotan exposure. [5,6] Baseline susceptibility, UV exposure, coincidental disease, and uncertain product composition complicate causal attribution. [3,5,6]

Mechanistic evidence is not uniformly cancer-promoting. α-MSH reduced UV-related DNA damage in cultured human melanocytes. [12] Topical MTII reduced implanted melanoma growth in a mouse model, with PTEN-related changes, reduced AKT/NF-κB/COX-2/PGE₂ signaling, and changes consistent with reduced proliferation and angiogenesis and increased apoptosis. [9]

Renal, muscular, and cardiovascular endpoints: Acute muscle injury, kidney dysfunction, tachycardia, and hypertension occurred in a toxicity case. [7] A separate renal-infarction report described persistent hypertension and mildly reduced renal clearance at two months. [19] These are serious safety signals; neither common-event incidence nor a favorable cumulative-exposure prediction can be estimated from these reports.

Neurological and sexual endpoints: Somnolence and fatigue occurred during short human testing. [1] A PRES report provides a serious neurological signal, while ischemic priapism can cause lasting erectile impairment. [8,17]

Endocrine and metabolic endpoints: A man with type 1 diabetes developed marked hyperglycaemia, ketosis without acidosis, hypertension, and transient hypercortisolism after intermittent use of a product labeled Melanotan 2. Product exposure was not analytically confirmed, and the case does not establish the proposed receptor or assay-interference explanations. [18]

Practical recommendations

  • Avoid unsupervised cosmetic MTII use, particularly with active cancer, prior melanoma, many atypical moles, or other substantial skin-cancer risk. This is precautionary advice rather than a validated drug-label contraindication list. [4–6,14]
  • After use, obtain clinical assessment of new or changing lesions. Tell the clinician about MTII exposure and UV exposure; the clinician can determine whether a full skin examination and ongoing surveillance are appropriate. Monitoring does not make continued use safe. [5,6]
  • Do not use MTII as a skin-cancer prevention strategy. Maintain ordinary sun protection and avoid intentional excess UV exposure. [4,12,15]
